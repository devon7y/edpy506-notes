Topic 4

Imbalanced classes

When one class is rare, a model can score almost perfectly and never once find it. The fixes here work on the training data, before the model sees it.

1What class imbalance is

Class imbalance occurs when there are many more instances of some classes than others, most often in binary classification. Fraud detection, loan default prediction, spam detection, anomaly detection and outlier detection are all imbalanced problems: the interesting case is the rare one.

Standard classification algorithms tend to be overwhelmed by the large class and ignore the small one. Most of them were designed around the assumption of an equal, or similar, number of cases in each class, and when that assumption fails the cheapest way to be right most of the time is to predict the common class.

On the same homes as the classification page, whether a home sells inside thirty days splits about evenly. In a slower market it does not. The homes stay the same, the relationship between price and speed of sale stays the same, and quick sales simply become rare.

2Where it comes from

Biased sampling

The imbalance was caused by how the examples were collected or sampled from the problem domain. The data are skewed because the collection was.

Errors in data collection

Mistakes made while collecting the observations, such as applying the wrong class label to many of them. If training data come from a narrow region or a narrow slice of time, one class may not be observed enough.

The problem domain

The imbalance is simply a property of the problem. One class naturally dominates: legitimate emails outnumber spam, and healthy people outnumber people with cancer. A slow housing market, where most homes do not sell in a month, is this kind.

3How severe it is

A slight imbalance is uneven by a small amount, say 40% against 60%, and is often not a concern: the problem can be treated like an ordinary classification problem. A severe imbalance is uneven by a large amount, say 1% against 99%, with hundreds or thousands of examples in one class and tens in the other, and can need specialised techniques.

The class with abundant examples is the majority class and the one with few is the minority class. The minority is usually the class of most interest, and it is the harder one to predict, because with fewer examples the model struggles to learn what they look like.

4Why accuracy stops working

Take credit card fraud, where perhaps 1% of transactions are fraudulent and 99% are legitimate. Three things go wrong at once.

  1. A biased model. Trained on the imbalanced data without addressing it, the model leans toward the majority class and predicts most transactions as legitimate, reaching a high accuracy while failing to detect fraud.
  2. Accuracy is misleading. A model that calls every transaction legitimate is 99% accurate and detects nothing.
  3. Rare class neglect. With very few fraudulent examples the model struggles to learn what fraud looks like, and misses subtle cases, producing many false negatives.

Under class imbalance, high accuracy can hide a model that is completely useless for the class you care about. Precision, recall and F1 on the minority class, not overall accuracy, are the right way to judge these models.

5Three approaches

Data-level methodsChange the collection of examples to balance the classes, or remove difficult samples
Algorithm-level methodsModify the learning algorithm itself to reduce its bias toward the majority class
Hybrid methodsCombine the two

Before any data-level method, check whether collecting more data is possible. A larger dataset with more minority examples shrinks the problem at its source. When cost or time rule that out, resampling builds a more balanced training set from the one available, by removing majority examples (undersampling) or generating new minority ones (oversampling).

6Random undersampling and random oversampling

Random undersampling removes majority examples at random until the two classes are a similar size. Random oversampling duplicates minority examples at random, drawing with replacement, until the classes are equal.

Random undersampling
  • + Cuts the excess majority examples
  • + Trains faster, on less data
  • − Can throw away helpful information
  • − Does nothing to improve the minority class
Random oversampling
  • + More minority examples to learn from
  • + Standard classifiers perform better
  • − Copies raise the risk of overfitting
  • − Trains slower, on more data

7SMOTE

Random oversampling duplicates minority examples but adds nothing the model can learn from: a copy of a point is the same point. Better to synthesise new examples from the existing ones. That is the Synthetic Minority Oversampling Technique, SMOTE, and its goal is new minority examples that resemble the real ones.

  1. Select a minority instance, A, at random.
  2. Find its k nearest minority-class neighbours, typically k = 5.
  3. Choose one of those neighbours, B, at random, and join A and B with a line segment.
  4. Place a new, synthetic instance at a random point along that segment.

SMOTE-NC, SMOTE for nominal and continuous features, is a variant that handles datasets mixing continuous and categorical features.

8The hybrid: SMOTE-NC with random undersampling

Two steps, one from each side:

  1. Synthesise minority examples from their nearest neighbours.
  2. Randomly remove majority examples, with or without replacement.

Advantages

  • Can handle categorical variables
  • Less computation, because some majority examples are removed

Disadvantages

  • Can throw away helpful information
  • Sub-optimal performance with high-dimensional datasets

10A rare disease

A hospital wants a model that flags patients likely to have a rare disease from their test results and symptoms. Of 1,000 patients screened, only 50 have it: a prevalence of 5%, the same imbalance as the severely imbalanced homes.

The stakes differ from fraud detection. A missed fraud case costs money, but a missed disease case can cost a life, so failing to catch the minority class is far more serious than a false alarm. Rare class neglect applies with full force: with only 50 positive cases to learn from, the model has very little signal about what the disease looks like, and may fail to recognise genuine cases, producing many false negatives.
